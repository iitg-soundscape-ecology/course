Part 1 · Guided reading — 15 minutes

1. Why quantify a soundscape?

A soundscape is the acoustic environment as it is experienced, observed or recorded in a particular place and time. Environmental recordings can contain biophony (sounds produced by living organisms), geophony (non-biological natural sounds such as wind and water) and anthrophony (sounds associated with human activity).

Long recordings are difficult to compare by listening alone. Indices condense selected properties of the audio or model detections into numerical values. They help describe patterns across sites and times, but they do not independently establish ecological condition, biodiversity or ecosystem health.

2. Two different approaches: acoustic indices and semantic indices

Conventional acoustic indices are calculated from properties of the audio signal, such as temporal variation in amplitude or the distribution of energy across frequency bands. Examples in ecoacoustic literature include ACI, ADI, AEI, BI and NDSI. Their definitions and implementation details can vary across software and parameter settings.

The indices used in the soundscape study for this course are semantic or detection-derived measures. They are calculated from PANNs sound-event predictions mapped to broad sound categories and BirdNET bird detections. The study reports seven measures, listed in the table below. Do not confuse these seven study measures with the conventional acoustic indices listed above.

3. The analysis workflow behind the study measures

  1. The recording is divided into short, overlapping analysis windows. In the current project configuration, each analysis window is 2 seconds, with a 1.5-second stride.
  2. PANNs analyses the audio and returns sound-event labels with confidence scores. The labels are mapped to broader categories, including Bird, Mammal, Amphibian, Insect, Water, Weather and Anthropogenic.
  3. BirdNET independently analyses the audio to identify candidate bird species and their temporal occurrences.
  4. BirdNET detections are cleaned and aligned with the PANNs analysis windows.
  5. The seven measures are calculated for each window and stored alongside detections, categories and temporal metadata.

PANNs and BirdNET provide complementary information: PANNs describes a broad range of acoustic events, while BirdNET provides bird-species-level detections.

Important distinction between AAI and HDI: AAI asks, “How much of the detected soundscape activity is anthropogenic?” HDI asks, “Within the anthropogenic component, how much is represented by the project's direct human-disturbance labels?” The direct-disturbance labels include speech/conversation, vehicles and road traffic, engines, aircraft/helicopters, trains, boats, music, radio and television.

5. How the measures complement one another

  • AAI and NSI describe the relative balance of anthropogenic and natural model-detected activity.
  • HDI provides a more specific view of the direct human-disturbance component within anthropogenic detections.
  • BAI indicates whether any bird detection occurred, while Richness and Shannon describe the detected species set and its distribution.
  • BDR expresses the number of BirdNET detections relative to the analysis-window duration.

Reading the measures together provides a richer description than relying on a single number. For example, a window can have BAI = 1 but low richness if only one species is detected.

6. Limitations and responsible interpretation

  • Model confidence is not physical sound energy. PANNs scores are model outputs; summing them creates a project-specific indicator, not a calibrated acoustic proportion.
  • Category mapping matters. Results depend on which AudioSet labels are assigned to natural or anthropogenic categories and which labels are designated as direct disturbance.
  • Detection is not presence/absence certainty. A zero BirdNET detection does not prove that birds were absent; quiet calls, masking, distance and model errors can affect detection.
  • Overlapping windows are not independent. With 2-second windows and a 1.5-second stride, adjacent windows share audio. Repeated detections across windows should not be treated as independent calls or population counts.
  • Window-level diversity is local in time. Shannon diversity and richness from a 2-second window can be sparse and sensitive to a single detection. Longer aggregations may be more stable, but should be reported clearly.
  • Indices are not ecosystem-health scores. Wind, rain, microphone characteristics, recorder gain, habitat structure and seasonal activity can influence the recording and model outputs.
