Part 1 · Guided reading — 15 minutes

1. What is digital signal processing?

A microphone converts changes in air pressure into an electrical signal. An analogue-to-digital converter samples that signal at regular intervals and represents each sample numerically. The resulting sequence is a digital audio signal. Digital signal processing (DSP) refers to operations applied to this sequence to inspect, transform or describe the sound.

In soundscape research, DSP helps answer questions such as: When does a sound occur? Which frequencies are prominent? Does the recording contain impulsive events, continuous noise or tonal calls? How do acoustic properties vary between locations or times of day?

A simplified workflow is: recording → quality check → preprocessing → segmentation → signal representation → feature extraction → interpretation. Machine-learning classifiers may use the waveform or derived representations, but the quality of their input still depends on recording and preprocessing decisions.

2. Digital audio: sampling, quantisation and amplitude

Sampling rate is the number of samples recorded per second, measured in hertz (Hz). A recording at 48 kHz contains 48,000 samples per second per channel. The Nyquist principle states that a sampling rate must be greater than twice the highest frequency one intends to represent, and practical recording also requires appropriate anti-alias filtering.

Bit depth determines how finely sample amplitudes are quantised. More bits provide more possible amplitude levels and a larger theoretical dynamic range. Bit depth does not increase the sampling rate or the maximum representable frequency.

Amplitude describes the numerical sample value. Unless the recording chain is calibrated, digital amplitude is not automatically a sound-pressure level in dB SPL. A waveform can be normalised for analysis or playback, but normalisation changes its numerical scale and must not be confused with calibrated measurement.

Example: A 10-second mono recording sampled at 48 kHz contains 480,000 samples. If stored as 16-bit PCM, the uncompressed audio payload is approximately 960,000 bytes, excluding file headers.

3. Inspect before processing

Before applying filters or extracting features, inspect the file's sample rate, number of channels, duration, encoding and amplitude range. Listen to the recording and inspect its waveform. Look for clipping (flat-topped peaks), long silent sections, abrupt dropouts, handling noise, wind bursts and electrical interference.

Keep the original recording unchanged. Save processed versions separately and document the operation, parameters and software version. This makes the analysis traceable and allows another researcher to reproduce it.

4. Preprocessing: what can be changed?

Channel conversion: Stereo can be retained when spatial information matters. A mono signal may be produced by averaging channels, but this can cause cancellation if channels are out of phase. Do not discard stereo automatically.

Resampling: Converts audio to a different sample rate by interpolation and filtering. It is not the same as changing the sample-rate label. Resample only when a tool or comparison requires a common rate, and use a proper resampling algorithm.

Filtering: A high-pass filter attenuates frequencies below a selected cutoff; a low-pass filter attenuates frequencies above it; a band-pass filter retains a selected frequency range. Filtering may reduce rumble or unwanted components, but it can also remove meaningful ecological information, such as low-frequency insect, amphibian, mammal or distant traffic sounds.

Normalisation: Scales amplitudes to a target peak or loudness. It can make files easier to compare or listen to, but it does not improve the signal-to-noise ratio and can amplify background noise. Avoid normalising recordings when absolute level differences are part of the research question.

Noise reduction: Attempts to suppress a noise component. Aggressive denoising may remove quiet bird calls, insect stridulation or other faint events. Always compare processed audio with the original and assess whether target sounds have been altered.

5. Segmentation: analysing a long recording in pieces

Environmental recordings may last minutes or hours. Many analysis methods operate on shorter segments called frames or windows. Segmentation makes processing manageable and allows sound activity to be tracked through time.

A window has a duration, while a hop or stride specifies how far the analysis moves before the next window begins. If the window is 2 seconds and the hop is 1.5 seconds, successive windows overlap by 0.5 seconds. Overlap can reduce the chance that a short event falls across a segment boundary, but neighbouring outputs are not statistically independent.

overlap = window duration − hop duration

For the course project workflow, each analysis record is associated with a 2-second window and a 1.5-second stride. Window start and end times should be retained so detections from different tools can be aligned in time.

6. Frequency analysis: Fourier transform and FFT

A waveform shows how amplitude changes over time. It does not directly show which frequencies make up a sound. The Fourier transform expresses a signal in terms of its frequency components. The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is the finite-sequence version used for digital signals; the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is an efficient algorithm for computing it.

A magnitude spectrum displays the strength of frequency components for a selected segment. It can help distinguish low-frequency rumble, tonal calls, broadband insect sounds and high-frequency components. However, a single spectrum does not show when each component occurred within a long recording.

7. Framing, windowing and the STFT

To examine how frequency content changes over time, the signal is divided into short, often overlapping frames. Each frame is multiplied by a window function (commonly Hann) before its FFT is calculated. The resulting sequence of spectra is the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT).

Windowing reduces spectral leakage caused by analysing a finite segment of a signal. The Hann window gradually tapers the frame at its edges. Window length creates a trade-off: longer windows offer finer frequency resolution but poorer time localisation; shorter windows localise rapid events more precisely but provide coarser frequency resolution.

STFT: X(m, k) = Σₙ x[n] · w[n − mH] · e−j2πkn/N

Here, x[n] is the audio signal, w is the window function, H is the hop length, N is the FFT size, m indexes time frames and k indexes frequency bins.

8. Spectrograms

A spectrogram visualises the magnitude or power of the STFT over time. Time is shown on the horizontal axis, frequency on the vertical axis, and colour or intensity represents signal energy. A spectrogram can reveal tonal bird calls, harmonic structures, broadband rain, repeated insect pulses, vehicle noise and transient events.

Interpretation depends on settings: frequency range, colour scale, dynamic range, window length and hop length. A visually bright region does not by itself identify a species or source. Spectrograms are descriptive representations, not ground-truth annotations.

9. Common audio features

Features summarise aspects of a signal and can be used for exploratory analysis, classification or comparison. They are not unique labels for sound sources; different sources can produce similar feature values.

10. MFCCs in brief

Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) are computed by taking short-time spectra, applying a bank of filters spaced on the Mel scale, taking logarithms of the filter-bank energies, and applying a discrete cosine transform. The resulting coefficients compactly describe the broad spectral envelope.

MFCCs are useful in machine-learning pipelines because they reduce a high-dimensional spectrum to a smaller feature vector. In environmental audio, they can help represent acoustic patterns, but they do not directly encode ecological categories. Their usefulness should be evaluated for the task and dataset rather than assumed.

11. Feature extraction is not ecological interpretation

A high spectral centroid does not prove that a sound is a bird; high-frequency wind or electronic noise may produce a similar result. High RMS energy does not necessarily mean high biodiversity. A feature becomes ecologically meaningful only when interpreted with context, validated annotations, recording conditions and an appropriate research question.

For this course project, conventional signal features can complement the parallel PANNs and BirdNET workflow. PANNs and BirdNET produce model-based sound-event and bird-species predictions; DSP features describe signal properties. These are related but distinct forms of evidence.

Part 2 · Practical activity — 30 minutes

Inspect a soundscape recording and extract features

Task: Use a short WAV recording (ideally 10–30 seconds) containing at least two types of environmental sound. You may use Audacity for visual inspection and Python with librosa for feature extraction.

  1. File inspection (5 min): Load the audio and record its duration, sample rate, channel count and data type. Listen once without processing.
  2. Waveform and quality (5 min): Plot the waveform. Identify silence, clipping, abrupt transients or possible handling/wind noise.
  3. Spectrogram (7 min): Compute and display a spectrogram. Identify one tonal or narrowband event and one broadband or noisy event, if present.
  4. Feature extraction (8 min): Calculate frame-wise RMS, ZCR, spectral centroid and MFCCs. Plot at least two features over time.
  5. Parameter comparison (5 min): Change the STFT window length or hop length. Note how the spectrogram and temporal detail change.

Illustrative Python workflow

Install the packages if needed: pip install librosa soundfile matplotlib numpy. Replace the filename with your own WAV file.

import librosa
import librosa.display
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

file_path = "soundscape.wav"
y, sr = librosa.load(file_path, sr=None, mono=True)

print("Sample rate:", sr)
print("Duration (s):", len(y) / sr)
print("Samples:", len(y))

# STFT and spectrogram
n_fft = 2048
hop_length = 512
D = librosa.stft(y, n_fft=n_fft, hop_length=hop_length,
                 window="hann")
S_db = librosa.amplitude_to_db(np.abs(D), ref=np.max)

plt.figure(figsize=(10, 4))
librosa.display.specshow(S_db, sr=sr, hop_length=hop_length,
                         x_axis="time", y_axis="hz")
plt.colorbar(format="%+2.0f dB")
plt.title("Soundscape spectrogram")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

# Frame-wise features
rms = librosa.feature.rms(y=y, frame_length=n_fft,
                          hop_length=hop_length)[0]
zcr = librosa.feature.zero_crossing_rate(
    y, frame_length=n_fft, hop_length=hop_length)[0]
centroid = librosa.feature.spectral_centroid(
    y=y, sr=sr, n_fft=n_fft, hop_length=hop_length)[0]
mfcc = librosa.feature.mfcc(y=y, sr=sr, n_mfcc=13,
                            n_fft=n_fft, hop_length=hop_length)

print("RMS frames:", rms.shape[0])
print("ZCR frames:", zcr.shape[0])
print("Centroid frames:", centroid.shape[0])
print("MFCC matrix:", mfcc.shape)

# Plot two features
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 3))
plt.plot(librosa.frames_to_time(np.arange(len(rms)), sr=sr,
                                hop_length=hop_length), rms)
plt.xlabel("Time (s)"); plt.ylabel("RMS")
plt.title("RMS energy over time"); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

plt.figure(figsize=(10, 3))
plt.plot(librosa.frames_to_time(np.arange(len(centroid)), sr=sr,
                                hop_length=hop_length), centroid)
plt.xlabel("Time (s)"); plt.ylabel("Hz")
plt.title("Spectral centroid over time"); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Key takeaways

  • DSP provides methods for inspecting, transforming and describing digital audio.
  • Preprocessing choices must be motivated by the research question and documented.
  • FFT gives a frequency representation; STFT provides a time-varying representation.
  • Window and hop lengths determine the temporal sampling and time-frequency trade-off.
  • Features such as RMS, ZCR, centroid and MFCCs describe signal properties; they do not independently identify ecological sources.
  • In soundscape analysis, preserve the original recording and validate interpretations against listening, context and annotations.
